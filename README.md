# Where Florida's water goes

Animated maps of Florida's rivers, springs, and sinks, from the north Florida springs belt to the St. Lucie: **[waterways.paperhurts.dev](https://waterways.paperhurts.dev/)**

- **Where does the rain go?** (`rain.html`) shows every mapped creek in Florida, all 90,000 km of them from the Perdido to the Keys, colored by where its water ends up: the Gulf, the Atlantic, a sink into the aquifer, or nowhere at all. The bigger streams load with the page; as you zoom in, the smaller creeks and lakes fill in, tile by tile. Rain falls on every creek shown and flows downstream, faster on bigger rivers. Tap a creek to send a drop down its whole path to the sea. Creek cards link to the St. Lucie, Lake Okeechobee, Rainbow River, and Santa Fe maps when a creek's water passes through them. Tap a swallet to see where a creek goes underground and where it rises again. Spring boils are sized by FDEP magnitude. Zoom-to views cover the springs belt and the older map's areas (Gainesville's sinks, the Santa Fe, the St. Johns, the St. Lucie) and the rest of the state, from Pensacola to the Everglades and the Keys. Links from spring cards on other maps (`rain.html#lon,lat`) open zoomed in on that spring.
- **The Apalachicola starts at a dam** (`apalachicola.html`) maps the whole Apalachicola-Chattahoochee-Flint basin, from Lake Lanier and Atlanta to Apalachicola Bay. Florida's biggest river starts at Jim Woodruff Dam on the Georgia line, where the Chattahoochee and the Flint meet, below the Corps of Engineers' dams and the farms and cities that draw on them. Water enters each river where its eight live USGS gauges say it's gained, pools behind the dams, and runs to the bay, where FWC's oyster beds are drawn. The state lines are dashed. A panel charts the flow into Florida since 1923; cards tell the Supreme Court case Florida lost in 2021 and the oyster harvest's closure and 2026 reopening.
- **The Santa Fe breathes groundwater** (`santa-fe.html`) animates river and spring flow from live USGS gauges. It shows the river vanishing at River Sink and returning at River Rise, dye traces from Gainesville's sinks, and an aquifer time slider that runs from before development to the present.
- **The Ocklawaha is still dammed for an unfinished canal** (`ocklawaha.html`) maps the Ocklawaha River from Moss Bluff and Silver Springs to the St. Johns. The Kirkpatrick (Rodman) Dam, built in 1968 for the Cross Florida Barge Canal, still floods 15 miles of it as Rodman Reservoir, though the canal was stopped in 1971. Water from Lake Griffin, Silver Springs (in spring color), and Orange Creek runs at six live USGS gauges' rates, slows and spreads across the reservoir, and leaves at the dam. Rings mark the FDEP springs under the reservoir, and the unfinished Eureka Lock and Dam, the barge canal's cut, and Buckman Lock are drawn. A panel charts Silver Springs' flow since 1933.
- **Rainbow River starts full grown** (`rainbow.html`) shows a river that rises out of the ground at full size. Clear water from about 20 spring vents and tannic Withlacoochee water from upstream meet at Dunnellon, spread through Lake Rousseau, and leave for the Gulf down the old river or the barge canal, all driven by six live USGS gauges. Groundwater drifts across Rainbow's springshed toward the head springs, and a panel charts sixty years of flow: the spring barely changes while the river swings.
- **The St. Lucie was plumbed to a lake** (`st-lucie.html`) follows the St. Lucie Canal (C-44), dug from 1916 to 1924, from Lake Okeechobee at Port Mayaca to the St. Lucie Lock, then down the South Fork and through the estuary to the St. Lucie Inlet. Lake water and the canal's own runoff move at the rates two live USGS gauges report. When the lake is lower than the canal, the canal runs backward, and the map shows its runoff dividing between the lake and the lock. Seawater comes in through the inlet and reaches up the estuary as far as the live salinity at Speedy Point and Steele Point says it does. A panel charts the lake's releases since 1931.

- **The Indian River Lagoon barely trades with the sea** (`indian-river.html`) maps the 156-mile lagoon behind the barrier islands from Ponce de Leon Inlet to Jupiter Inlet, shaded by how far its water is, by water, from the nearest of its five inlets. The farthest, up the Banana River, is about 50 miles from one, and water there takes months to trade with the sea. The lagoon's own water fills it as faint specks that the tide sloshes in and out near each inlet, carrying some out to sea, while far from one only the wind stirs them. Seawater drops come in through the inlets and turn back within a few miles, and fresh water pours in from the creeks and drainage canals at their live USGS rates (the St. Sebastian River, Turkey and Crane creeks, the Eau Gallie, and the Fellsmere and Vero Beach canals). Haulover Canal runs whichever way the wind and tide push it. A panel charts the canals' and creeks' water-year flow since 1993. Tap the lagoon for how far you are from an inlet.

- **The Kissimmee got its bends back** (`kissimmee.html`) maps the Kissimmee River from Lake Kissimmee to Lake Okeechobee as it is now: C-38, the canal dug from 1962 to 1971, still open below Lake Kissimmee and above Lake Okeechobee, and the winding river between, where 22 miles of the canal were filled by 2021. Water runs fast down the canal, slow through the bends, and spreads over the floodplain marsh where the last stretch of canal was filled; the marsh glints. It flows at the live rate the Corps of Engineers' CWMS reports at S-65E, where the river enters the lake (USGS stopped gauging it in 2004), with Lake Istokpoga's outlet (S-68) coming in down Canal C-41A. A panel charts the flow into the lake since 1929.

- **Lake Okeechobee used to drain south** (`lake-o.html`) shows where Florida's biggest lake sends its water now that canals and a dike have replaced its spill south into the Everglades: west down the Caloosahatchee, east down the St. Lucie Canal, and south into four farm canals. Each gauged outlet runs at its live USGS rate, and lake water drifts toward whichever gates are open. Fisheating Creek flows in at its live rate. A panel charts the water-year flow each way since 1932, including the years the south canals pumped farm runoff back into the lake.

- **Florida's Coral Reef lost its builders** (`reefs.html`) maps the reef from the St. Lucie Inlet to the Dry Tortugas: the reef tract, 15,000 patch reefs, hard bottom, seagrass, and artificial reefs, from FWC's Unified Florida Reef Map. The reefs glint in the color of NOAA Coral Reef Watch's latest heat stress alert for their region (the Florida Keys or Southeast Florida), refreshed with every deploy. A panel charts each year's peak heat stress since 1985, and the text tells how the reef lost pillar, staghorn, and elkhorn coral. Tap a reef for what it is and today's heat there.

- **Florida's springs and snorkel spots** (`springs.html`) maps every spring on FDEP's statewide list (the journal's list, 889 after merging vents), sized by magnitude, with the first- and second-magnitude springs boiling. The areas FDEP's 13 springs cleanup plans (BMAPs) cover, and their priority focus areas, are shaded. Search by name, zoom to a region, or show only the biggest springs; cards link to the rain map or the Rainbow page where a spring appears there. Coral marks places to snorkel, springs and not: a ring around the snorkeling springs, and a diamond for the reefs, jetties, lagoon spots, parks, and sinkholes (`config/snorkel.json`, located with OpenStreetMap), from the Panhandle to the Keys. Every bay, lagoon, and tidal river is drawn as water, including the ones the Census coastline counts as land (the Indian River Lagoon, Choctawhatchee and Pensacola bays, Tampa Bay). The named coastal lagoons (the Indian River, Mosquito, Lake Worth, Biscayne Bay, Sarasota Bay, Santa Rosa Sound, and others) have labels and cards. Florida's lakes, wetlands (glinting as the sun catches their water), and the rain map's creeks sit under the springs, the smaller creeks filling in as you zoom, and the 60 springs the USGS maps that FDEP doesn't list are drawn as unrated dots. A spring's card names the state park it's in and links to it on the parks map.

- **Florida's state parks keep its water** (`parks.html`) maps all 177 state parks, trails, and historic sites in FDEP's park boundaries, each shaded by the water it protects: springs, rivers, lakes and wetlands, coast, or reef, from FDEP's map of the parks' natural communities. The parks glint in their water's color. FDEP's springs are bright inside a park and faint outside one, and the parks' first-magnitude springs boil. The lede counts the springs in parks (137 of 889) and the first-magnitude springs (8 of 26, counting a spring's vents once). Only two parks hold living reef: John Pennekamp's coral and St. Lucie Inlet's worm reef. Filter by water, zoom to a region, or search by name; `parks.html#<park-slug>` opens on a park. Cards give its acreage, its biggest water communities, its springs, a link to its Florida State Parks page, and, for the nine parks in DEP's 2024 plan for golf courses and lodges, what became of it.

- **Spring journal** (`journal.html`) is a private, shared log of Florida spring and snorkel visits. It covers all 889 springs in FDEP's statewide list, the curated snorkel spots, and any spot a member adds (by GPS, pasted coordinates, or a tap on the map). Each visit records a date, a 1–5 rating, notes, photos, and wildlife sightings; at salt-water spots the quick-tap animals switch to reef and lagoon ones. A sighting can be pinned by the phone's GPS when you tap it. The journal map shows visits and sightings over USGS satellite or topo imagery, with a layer per animal group that you can switch on and off. Signed-in members also see their visits and sightings on the public maps. A spring's sheet names the state park it's in and links there.

```

  lake-o.html, st-lucie.html, reefs.html, journal.html   page shells (Vite entry points)
src/shared/     projection, pan/zoom viewport, streak renderer, lakes, flow-history chart, site nav, theme tokens, data types

src/santa-fe/   the Santa Fe map: flow model, aquifer grids, authored content
src/rainbow/    the Rainbow River map: flow model, authored content
src/st-lucie/   the St. Lucie map: canal and salinity model, authored content
src/lake-o/     the Lake Okeechobee map: outlet model, authored content
src/springs/    the statewide springs map
src/parks/      the state parks map: authored content, park names
src/indian-river/, src/reefs/   the Indian River Lagoon and coral reef maps

tests/          data-integrity checks on public/data
```

## Develop

```bash
npm install
npm run dev
```

The dev server runs at http://localhost:5180 (preview at 4180). Both ports are pinned with `strictPort`, so Vite fails loudly instead of drifting onto another project's port.

```bash

```

## Regenerate the data

The pipeline needs [uv](https://docs.astral.sh/uv/). Downloads are cached in `pipeline/cache/`, so reruns are fast. The rain map and the newer map areas read USGS's bulk NHDPlus HR files, about 50–150 MB zipped per 4-digit HUC (eight for the whole state), downloaded there on first use. Pass `--refresh` to refetch everything.

```bash
cd pipeline
uv run waterways all              # or one: rain | springs | parks | statewide | reefs | snapshot | …
uv run --group dev pytest
```

Then run `npm test` from the repo root to check the output before committing it.

## Spring journal

The journal runs on a free Supabase project. `config/supabase.json` holds its URL and publishable key. Both are public by design: every table and the photo bucket use row-level security, and all access requires a signed-in email on the `members` list. Members can read everything. Each person can edit or delete only their own entries. Photos are resized and re-encoded in the browser before upload, which strips their EXIF GPS tags.

- **Sign in:** members get an emailed link. In Supabase → Authentication → URL Configuration, set the Site URL to `https://waterways.paperhurts.dev/journal.html` and add `http://localhost:5180/journal.html` as a redirect URL.
- **Invite people:** add them under People in the journal; they sign in from the journal page with that email. Sign-in links go out through a custom SMTP sender (Resend), because Supabase's built-in email only reaches its own team. A "Before User Created" auth hook refuses accounts for anyone not on the list, so the form can't email strangers.
- **Schema:** SQL lives in `supabase/migrations/`. The first member is seeded by hand, so no personal email is committed.
- **Staying awake:** Supabase pauses free projects after a week without activity. The six-hourly deploy calls a no-op `ping()` to prevent that. If the journal ever says it can't load, restore the project from the Supabase dashboard.
- **Developing without an account:** `http://localhost:5180/journal.html?demo` shows sample data and saves nothing. It's dev-only and stripped from production builds.

## Deploy

`.github/workflows/deploy.yml` builds and publishes to GitHub Pages on every push to `main` and every six hours. Each run fetches fresh gauge readings into the deployed snapshot, and nothing is committed. GitHub pauses scheduled workflows in public repos after 60 days without commits. If the snapshot goes stale, re-enable the workflow from the Actions tab.

Pages must be set to deploy from **GitHub Actions** (Settings → Pages → Source). The custom domain `waterways.paperhurts.dev` is set in the Pages settings. DNS is a CNAME record, `waterways` → `paperhurts.github.io`, at the domain's registrar. The old `paperhurts.github.io/waterways` address redirects to it. The repo carries the GitHub topic `paperhurts-dev`, which puts a card for it on the [paperhurts.dev](https://paperhurts.dev) homepage. The card copy lives in `projects.config.json` in `paperhurts/paperhurts.github.io`.

## Data sources
- Streams, rivers, lakes, and sink/spring points: USGS NHDPlus High Resolution, from [hydro.nationalmap.gov](https://hydro.nationalmap.gov/) and, for the newer areas, USGS's [bulk file geodatabases](https://prd-tnm.s3.amazonaws.com/index.html?prefix=StagedProducts/Hydrography/NHDPlusHR/Beta/GDB/) (the same release)
- Coastline: US Census Bureau cartographic boundary states (1:500,000), via [TIGERweb](https://tigerweb.geo.census.gov/)
- Journal basemaps: USGS The National Map (imagery, topo, hydrography)
- River and spring discharge, and estuary salinity: [USGS Water Data API](https://api.waterdata.usgs.gov/) (instantaneous and daily values)
- Springs: FDEP Florida Springs layer
- Oyster beds: FWC FWRI, Oyster Beds Statewide
- Kissimmee River structures (S-65E, S-68): SFWMD readings through the US Army Corps of Engineers' [CWMS Data API](https://cwms-data.usace.army.mil/cwms-data/); C-38's backfilled stretches: © OpenStreetMap contributors (ODbL)
- State parks: FDEP Division of Recreation and Parks, park boundaries and natural communities (FNAI's classification)
- Reef habitat: FWC FWRI Unified Florida Reef Map; heat stress: NOAA Coral Reef Watch
- Snorkel spot locations: © [OpenStreetMap](https://www.openstreetmap.org/copyright) contributors (ODbL), found with Nominatim
- Springsheds: Southwest Florida Water Management District (Major Springsheds); springs cleanup plan (BMAP) and priority focus areas: FDEP Statewide BMAP layers
- Swallets: Florida Geological Survey swallet survey
- Aquifer: FDEP / FGS Upper Floridan Aquifer potentiometric surface, including the USGS pre-development and historic surfaces
- Dye traces: Karst Environmental Services, Mill Creek and Lee Sinks Dye Trace (2005), for Alachua County EPD

## Known limits
- Individual springs are mostly ungauged. Spring dots split each reach's measured gain evenly.
- Some historic flows are scaled from the Fort White gauge and marked "est.".
- Aquifer maps come from different agencies and methods at 10-ft contour intervals. Compare shapes between years, not single feet. The grid is less certain near its edges, which the map fades out.
- "Ends inland" can mean a sink, a closed wetland, a lake with no outlet, or a gap in the map.
- NHD routes Rose Creek and the Santa Fe through underground conduits, so their water counts toward the Gulf. The map labels the swallets where they go underground.
- On the St. Lucie map, only the canal's two structures are gauged live. The North Fork and its canals (C-23, C-24) are metered by the South Florida Water Management District, whose data service requires credentials it issues, so the map draws them without animating their flow. The tide isn't gauged either: seawater drops show how far the salt reaches, from the two salinity stations, interpolated between them.
- On the Lake Okeechobee map, the Kissimmee (USGS stopped gauging it at the lake in 2004) and the West Palm Beach Canal (2008) are drawn without animated flow, and the lake's own level isn't in USGS's live data. Lake water drifting to the gates shows which way it's leaving, not its path.
- On the parks map, a park's water is what's inside its boundary. Most parks' lines stop at the shore, so the lake, river, or bay a park is named for often isn't counted; lake parks are placed by name, and coastal parks by a tie-break toward the coast.
- On the rain map, drops merge as streams join, so big rivers show fewer but heavier drops. The totals in the legend are measured by creek length, not by drop count.
